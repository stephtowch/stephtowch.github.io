The International Work Addiction Scale (IWAS) **[(Charzyńska et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1556/2006.2025.00005)** is a brief measure of work addiction developed from the Bergen Work Addiction Scale **[(Andreassen et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9450.2012.00947.x)** and based on the  the components model of addiction **[(Griffiths, 2005)](https://doi.org/10.1080/14659890500114359)**. 

The scale assesses seven core components of addiction:
1) **Salience** – work becomes the most important activity in a person's life
2) **Mood modification** – work is used to change or regulate mood
3) **Tolerance** – increasingly more work is needed to achieve the same effects
4) **Withdrawal** – unpleasant feelings occur when unable to work
5) **Conflict** – work interferes with other areas of life
6) **Relapse** – returning to excessive working after attempts to reduce it
7) **Problems** – continued excessive working despite negative consequences

There are two versions of the IWAS:
- **IWAS-7** – includes all seven components
- **IWAS-5** – a shorter version that excludes tolerance and withdrawal and was developed particularly for cross-cultural research

## Cross-Cultural Devlopement

Charzyńska and colleagues (2025) found that the IWAS-7 was invariant across 81 cultures, while the IWAS-5 was invariant across 85 cultures. This means that the scales measured work addiction in a comparable way across these cultural groups, allowing scores to be meaningfully compared. Higher work addiction scores were also associated with greater job stress, lower job satisfaction, and lower self-esteem across cultures. Across the 85 cultures included in the study, the mean prevalence of work addiction was 17.9%.

Cut-off scores were established for both versions:

- **IWAS-7**: ≥24
- **IWAS-5**: ≥18

## UK Validation

A subsequent validation study examined the psychometric properties of the IWAS in a nationally representative sample of employed adults in the United Kingdom **[(Towch et al., 2026)](https://doi.org/10.1177/10731911261447068).** 

The IWAS-7 demonstrated strong validity and reliability and was invariant across several important demographic and occupational groups, including gender, age, managerial role, education, and work sector. There were no differences in latent work addiction levels between men and women or between public-sector, private-sector and self-employed workers, although differences were observed across age, education and managerial-role groups.

An item response theory (IRT) analysis also found that all seven items had very high discrimination, indicating that they effectively distinguished between people with different levels of work addiction. The scale provided its greatest measurement precision at moderate to high levels of work addiction, rather than at the extreme ends of the trait. The withdrawal, conflict, and problems items provided particularly high levels of information, suggesting that these components may be especially informative for distinguishing between different levels of work addiction severity.

Together, these findings provide further evidence that the IWAS is a psychometrically robust measure of work addiction and is suitable for use in working populations.

## Availability

The IWAS is freely available and can be used by researchers interested in work addiction. Its brief format makes it particularly useful for research where questionnaire length, participant time, or other resources are limited.

The IWAS has been translated into 68 languages, supporting its use in international and cross-cultural research.
